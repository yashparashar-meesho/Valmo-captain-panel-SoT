Keeping this honest

This pack is only worth anything if it stays true. The rule:

When someone is corrected, the correction lands in a file — not in a chat, and not in an assistant's memory.

A correction kept in a session reaches one person once. The same correction in never-do-this.md or fidelity-checklist.md reaches everyone who ever pulls a screen. fidelity-checklist.md is literally a correction log, and it is the most valuable file here for exactly that reason.

docs/panel-principles.md — Who the captain is

The captain, and the panel's standing problems

From the Captain Panel — Increasing Trust, Comprehension and Adoption workshop. This is the only file in the pack that describes the person and the product, rather than how to build a page. Read it in the brief, before anything else — most of it changes design decisions, and several entries contradict assumptions a page can easily be built on.

Who the captain is

A seasoned logistics partner running a small-to-medium delivery centre. Not an office worker. Their day is physical, interrupted, and spent across several tools — most of which are not this panel.

whenwhat they are doingthe tool they reach for
early morningsorting shipments, assigning pilotsLog10, physical sorting, WhatsApp
mid-daytracking RVP pickups, watching OOROpsTech, WhatsApp
eveningcash collection, reconciliationExcel / register, Log10, WhatsApp
end of dayreporting, manpower planningCaptain Panel, Libra, AM calls

That table is today. Design for where it is going.

The panels are merging. Log10, OpsTech and the ERP panel are being pulled into the Captain Panel, and daily operations move onto it. So the table above describes a captain who is being replaced by a different one: not someone who visits at the end of the day to report, but someone working inside the panel all day, under operational pressure.

Design for that captain. Concretely, it means:

  • A page may assume the captain is in the panel while the work happens. Time-critical things can live here. They could not before.
  • Speed is a feature. The work is scanning, manifesting, closing DRS, handling exceptions, reconciling — high volume, repeated, under time pressure. Captains already cross 3–5 tabs for a single task. Every extra step is paid many times a day.
  • Read-only is not enough. "Most things are read only" is named as the root of the adoption problem. A page that only displays has to say why it is not simply a report.
  • The panel is becoming the place of record. As operations move onto it, a number that disagrees with another surface is not a cosmetic bug.

Two things that do not change with the merge

WhatsApp is the competitor, and it is winning. "WhatsApp pe AM ko message karna faster hai." A page justifies itself by beating a message to a human. "We put it on the panel" is not a solution on its own — and merging the panels raises that bar rather than removing it.

The captain is busy and interrupted. "I'm usually busy in operations — I miss the training sessions." Nothing can assume prior training, a tutorial seen once, or a term explained elsewhere. Recall is not available, and an all-day tool under pressure makes that more true, not less.

The eight principles

From the Loadshare panel UX audit. Use them as the UX checks on a finished design — each one answered with an argument, not a tick.

principlethe question to answer
1EfficiencyHow many steps and tabs does the task take? Captains cross 3–5 tabs per task today.
2Proactive communicationDoes it tell them before the problem, or after? Deadlines and capacity limits are invisible today until an action is blocked.
3GuidanceIs help at the point of need, or somewhere else?
4TransparencyIs system state visible — what happened, what is happening, what will?
5ActionabilityCan they fix it from here, and is the next step obvious?
6ConsistencySame word, same placement, same pattern as the rest of the panel.
7IntuitivenessCan it be recognised at a glance, without reading?
8Error preventionAre inputs validated early? Is there a fallback when the system fails?

Known failures — never ship these again

Every one of these was found in the live panel. They are not hypothetical.

Copy and terminology

  • Use the captain's word, not the system's. They say OFD and OOR; the panel says Open Orders. They say DC; other surfaces say Hub.
  • "Capacity Limit" and "DO Compliance" are not understood. Neither is "prevent" in "days left to prevent loss".
  • "Loss marked" reads as already processed. It is not.
  • The same thing must be called the same thing on Atlas, the panel, Log10 and in offline comms. Inconsistency across surfaces is itself a trust problem.

Components

  • An empty cell shown as – reads as a loading failure. Design the empty state; never leave a dash standing in for one.
  • Selected and unselected states are not distinguishable. This has been reported from the live panel, so a tab strip or toggle that only changes weight is not enough.
  • The info i icon is too small to be seen or hit.
  • Hover-only actions are not discoverable. Anything hover-revealed is, in practice, missing.
  • Do not mix pagination and infinite scroll. Pick one, panel-wide.
  • Horizontal scroll is hard to use — Loss Management is the example. Avoid it; if unavoidable, make it obvious.
  • Informational and actionable elements are not visually separated. If a captain cannot tell what they can press, they press nothing.

Structure

  • There is no homepage. The panel has no starting point, so no page can assume the captain arrived from anywhere in particular.
  • No hierarchy or grouping in navigation, and no framework for adding new features — which is why every new module makes the rail worse.
  • Nudges and flags compete. Several modules each shout; nothing says which is urgent. A new flag needs a reason to outrank the others, and a sunset.

Trust and data

  • Payments lag by a week; some data by 8–10 hours. A number without a timestamp will be mistrusted, and rightly.
  • Loadshare and the panel disagree on volume and payments. If a figure exists elsewhere, say which source this one is.
  • Most of the panel is read-only, which is the root of the adoption problem. A page that only displays has to justify why it is not a report.

The communication rule

If it's not on the panel, it's not official.

The panel is the intended single source of truth. Critical updates land here first; WhatsApp and AMs are reminder channels that point back, never replacements. A design that assumes an AM will explain it has already failed.

What is changing, and must not be designed against

  • Typography is moving from Mier B02 to DM Sans. The catalogue is still Mier and is correct today. Do not pre-empt the change; do not add anything that depends on Mier's specific metrics.
  • The colour palette is being revised for contrast and accessibility — the current primary blue sits too close to dark grey.
  • Mobile web is coming. Captains are already using the panel on phones with no mobile experience at all. A desktop-only layout is a known gap, not a safe assumption.

These are directional and dated. If a design turns on one of them, ask — do not assume this file is current.

How a problem becomes a page. Four sections, and every one of them stops for a human before the next begins.

This file is the instruction set behind the slipstream skill. Reading it is enough to run the method by hand; the skill just removes the fetching.

The loop underneath

The shipped module is now in the catalogue, so the next Section 0 starts from a better map. That is the whole point of the dashed line — and it only keeps working while promoting stays cheap. The moment it becomes a chore, the catalogue starts going stale and Section 0 starts lying.

Section 0 — Locate

Reading only — nothing is fetched. Ends on a gate: the brief is shown and agreed before Section 1 starts.

Section 0 is reading. The pack is all you need — no screen files, no icons, no fonts, no stylesheet. Those come in one batched pass later, once the DRD has been approved. Fetching early spends the user's time on files that may belong to a page they are about to rule out.

Before anything is designed, establish what already exists. Skipping this is how approaches get invented in a vacuum and the final UI lands off-spec.

  1. Read panel-principles.md first — who the captain is, and the panel's standing failures. Several entries there contradict assumptions a page is easy to build on: the panels are merging, so design for a captain working inside this one all day under operational pressure, not the end-of-day reporter they are today; WhatsApp is the competitor; there is no homepage, so nothing can assume where they arrived from.
  2. Read pages.md and components.md.
  3. Match the KRD against them.
  4. Sweep for the cases the KRD did not mention. A KRD describes the happy path almost every time. The edge cases are not missing because they do not matter — they are missing because nobody thought of them, and that includes you unless you work from a list:
generatorask
datamissing · zero · stale · exactly one · thousands · exactly at the threshold
timethe first ever · mid-transition · after a long absence · the current, incomplete period
permissionthe user cannot act · a different role is looking
lifecyclebefore · during · after · reverted

Run it and the capacity-cut KRD immediately gives up: exactly at 2pp · a new DC that is exempt · already at the 100-order floor · every metric green but no new FE · this week's data incomplete. The KRD covers none of them. Each one becomes either a constraint or a success test, and each one has to appear as a situation in the shapes.

  1. Write the brief — BRIEF.md, then render it and open it:

``bash python3 scripts/render_doc.py BRIEF.md ``

partwhat it holds
the problem as you read itin your words, not the KRD's — this is the part that gets corrected
what it touchesevery module and screen the problem reaches, named so none can vanish later
constraints C1…the rules the design cannot break
success tests T1…what a person must be able to do, or the design has failed
situations S1…every case the sweep found, the happy path among them
open questionswhat only the reader can answer, and what you will assume if they do not

Everything downstream references these by name. That is the point: an approach is checked against T2, a shape is marked against S5.

Nothing else goes in it. The conventions that bind the work and the components you can reuse were both established in step 2 — keep them, you will need them in Section 2 — but they are build material, and the brief is not for the builder. It is read by someone deciding whether you have understood their problem, and every paragraph that is not that is a paragraph between them and the answer.

A brief is read, corrected and handed back. If it cannot be done in a few minutes it will be skimmed, and a skimmed brief is approved without being checked — which is worse than no gate at all.

  1. Gate — the brief is good to go. Show it and ask, in those words. Nothing in Section 1 starts until the person who asked for the work says so.

The cheapest correction in the method, and the most valuable. A misread problem produces a correct-looking DRD, five careful approaches and a finished design that solves the wrong thing — and nothing downstream catches it, because every later stage inherits the misreading.

In the capacity-cut run the brief came back with three corrections: ignore AMs, the week renders to yesterday, this is capacity cuts only. The first removed an entire approach, because that approach existed to be read by two people. Arriving after the DRD, those corrections would have invalidated five approaches already argued in full.

IDs freeze here. Before this gate, renumber freely. After it, C4 and T2 are referenced by name across every later document — mark withdrawn items withdrawn, and give new facts new numbers.

The prerequisite: understand the problem properly first. This is not a step, it is the ground everything else stands on. A misread problem produces a correct-looking DRD, five careful approaches and a finished design that solves the wrong thing — and nothing downstream will catch it, because every later stage inherits the misreading. If the KRD is thin, the right move is to go back to it, not to proceed carefully.

Do not decide where it lives here. Where a thing goes is one of the things the five approaches differ on — settling it before they exist collapses the design space to whatever seemed obvious first, and the DRD then diverges inside a box nobody argued for. It belongs in the DRD, and the question belongs in the DRD's open questions.

Section 1 — Diverge

You drive. The DRD is a blocking gate: nothing is built until it is approved.

This was non-blocking in the first version, to protect cycle time. After a real run it was changed: the DRD was skipped, wireframes were built against nothing written down, and an approach nobody had considered turned out to be the right one. The gate is worth the day.

  1. Interrogate the gaps — ask only what the KRD left out and Section 0 could not answer. Do not re-ask what the catalogue already knows.

A question about the KRD is about what the KRD does not say. A question about the design is yours to decide and state. Mixing them makes the reader adjudicate your design choices as though they were facts. Order questions by how much design they change, and always say what you will do if they go unanswered.

  1. Check the brief still holds. It was approved in Section 0, so its constraints, success tests and situations are fixed and referenced by name from here on. If something in it is now wrong, fix it there and say so — never carry a second version of the truth in the DRD.
  1. Write the DRD — DRD.md as the source, then render it to a page and open it in the browser:

``bash python3 scripts/render_doc.py DRD.md ``

Handing over a raw .md hands over the source of a document, not the document. It reads badly, and approving it feels like reviewing a diff.

Every approach entry carries the same five parts, in this order:

partwhat it is for
What the captain doesthe numbered journey — see step 6
one or two lineswhat the thing actually is
Wins when · Costs · Rules out · Leans onthe trade, compressed
Why it can workthe argument for it, taken seriously
What would make it failthe one thing that would sink it

And the set gets checked against the brief's success tests — which approach answers T1, which only half-answers T2. A table does it in a glance; the form matters less than the fact that an approach nobody has checked against the tests is an opinion.

Argue every approach as though it were the one being built. An approach written only as a trade-off line is a straw man, and a set of straw men is not a choice — it is the recommendation with scenery. The reader must be able to disagree with you from the page itself.

Keep each to a short paragraph. Detail earns its place by changing somebody's mind; past that it is bulk, and bulk does not get read.

  1. As many approaches as the problem actually has. Not five for the sake of five. For each: what it does, what it costs, what it rules out, and where it would live.

### Approaches are derived from the brief, not invented

Do not think of approaches and then check them against the axes. By the time you have thought of three, you have already anchored on whichever came to mind first, and the check only confirms it. Build the candidate set out of the lists the brief already wrote:

axiswhere its span comes from
where it livesevery module the brief's what it touches named. Each one is a candidate approach until ruled out in writing.
who actsevery actor the brief extracted — the user, another role, the system acting and then informing
when it is surfacedthe moments in the brief's situations: before the event · at the decision · on the event · after it
what is showneverything · only the exception · only the action
how it is framedstatus · warning · instruction · explanation · score — and add one if the problem admits another
depthsummary · item level · both

This is why it is generative rather than a checklist: the brief named the modules, the actors and the moments, so the candidates already exist. You are selecting and pruning, not imagining.

Every module named in what it touches appears in the approach table, or is ruled out in a sentence. Nothing from the brief may silently vanish. In the capacity-cut run, Growth Dashboard was named in the brief and then simply absent from the approaches — not rejected, absent — and nothing caught it but a human reading the table.

Placement is one axis, not the excluded one. An earlier version of this file said placement was "the entry axis, not the design axis", and the next run stopped varying placement at all. Vary placement and the rest. A set that moves only on placement is thin; a set that never moves on it has thrown away a whole dimension.

Place every approach on the table and show it. A set clustered on one point is visibly thin, which is the entire purpose of writing it down.

The discriminator. Two approaches are the same if switching from one to the other does not change what the user does. If their behaviour is identical, it is a variant, not an approach. Same look, different behaviour: two approaches. Different look, same behaviour: one.

### How many

The count follows the problem. If the honest answer is two, write two — and say why there are only two. Padding to five produces three real ideas and two costumes, and the costumes waste the reader's attention in exactly the place it is most needed.

If they cluster, stop and ask. When three approaches turn out to be the same idea in different clothes, say so, name the real fork underneath them, and ask which side of it to explore. A clustered set is not a failure to report — it usually means the problem has one obvious shape and a single real question, and naming that question is worth more than five options.

  1. Ask where it should live in the DRD's open questions, with your recommendation and the one reason that decides it. By then the approaches are on the page, so the question gets answered against something real instead of in the abstract.

Where it can live

There are only ever two answers, and the first has three placements inside it. Every approach in the DRD names its own placement — that is part of what makes the approaches different from one another.

1 — In an existing module. Name which one, and why that one. The three placements are a ladder, not a menu: each rung costs more than the one below it, so take the lowest rung that genuinely serves the problem.

Before proposing any new surface at all, ask whether a control removes the need for one. A date dropdown, a filter, a toggle or a sort on what is already there will often do the whole job. "Show me last week" is a date control, not a screen. A new tab or page proposed where a filter would have done is work nobody needed, in a place nobody will look.

Depth costs visibility, and that is a real trade — say it out loud. A nested page is seen by whoever goes looking for it. If you recommend 1c, name that cost in the same breath: "this fits here, but it will be seen less because it sits inside."

It is a cost to state, never grounds to rule a placement out — not even when the problem itself is one of awareness. If the placement is the best fit and everyone agrees the visibility is enough for what is being built, it ships. The method's job is to put the trade in front of the person deciding, not to decide for them by quietly removing an option.

placementwhen it is right
1aon the existing page — stack another section vertically, or extend a section already there, horizontally or vertically (a column, a field, a state)it fits on that page
1ba tab or sub-tabit does not fit on that page — adding to or removing from it stops making sense — but it belongs to the same subject, so a tab lets the module hold both
1cinside a nested page the module already hasit belongs to one item's detail, not to the module's main view

When two modules both seem to own it. This is common — pilot productivity touches capacity and the roster. It is not a reason to reach for a new module, and it is never a reason to build the thing twice.

  1. Pick the module where it makes more sense to live. That is its home, and it is built once, there.
  2. At the other touchpoint, add a door — an entry point, or a line explaining the new thing and linking to it. That keeps the two subjects correlated and sends traffic to the home.
  3. Never build the same thing in both places. Two homes means two things to maintain and, before long, two versions of the truth.
  4. If you genuinely cannot pick, say so and ask — and give your reasoning for each side, not just the question. A decision made with the reasoning in the open is one the next person can learn from; a bare "which one?" teaches nobody anything.

2 — A new module in the rail. Only when no module owns the subject. Expensive: it has to earn a permanent slot, which usually means someone opens it as part of a routine.

A module having no tabs today is not an argument against a tab. Any module can take one — the component exists, and which modules happen to use it now says nothing about which may. The same goes for sub-tabs and nested pages. Judge by whether the thing fits the page, never by the page's current furniture.

Within 1a, section-rules.md decides new section from extend what is there — own heading plus own data scope is the test.

Worked example — "show the capacity cut history, last five weeks". Both of the obvious answers are wrong, and for the same reason.

readinganswer it suggestswhy it is wrong
it is about the whole DC, not one itema tabif the ask is only show the weeks, a section on the page already there carries it — or it goes at the top of the first section. A tab buys nothing.
you would reach it from the "cut this week" carda nested pageif the overview has a date control, clicking any week already shows that week. The page is not needed at all.

The answer is 1a, and the thing that decides it is the size of the problem, not the shape of the navigation. Both wrong answers come from reaching for a new surface before asking whether the existing one could carry it.

  1. Give every approach the captain's journey. Not a description of the screen — the numbered steps the captain actually takes, from what sets them off to the point where they know what to do.
stepwhat it names
1what starts it — a notice, a habit, a task they were already doing
2where they land
3what they read
4what they do next
5what they now know

Write it before the trade-offs. It is the thing that exposes a shape that cannot finish — and a shape reads as plausible for exactly as long as nobody walks through it.

In the capacity-cut run, approach B ended at "They stop there. Nothing on the page says how to undo it." That one line ruled it out faster than its test coverage did.

The journey appears twice, word for word: in the DRD entry, and beside the schematic in Section 2. If drawing the shape changes the journey, the DRD is updated too.

  1. Do not converge yet. Converging here is what makes the whole exercise theatre: the shapes are compared as sentences, when the thing that actually decides it is seeing them laid out.

Approach ≠ IA. Diverge here on what we do about the problem — surface it on an existing screen, give it a dedicated page, make it a notification. Structure comes next. Diverging on both axes at once gives fifteen options that are really three, and nobody can review that.

Gate — the DRD is approved. Hard stop. Not "shared", not "sent" — approved, in words, by the person who asked for the work. Revise and re-render until they say it is good to go.

Only then does anything get fetched or built.

Every answer goes back into the artefacts

An answer given in chat is not a decision until it is written down. The moment the user answers anything — a gap, an open question, a correction — update the brief and the DRD before doing anything else, and show what changed.

Three reasons this is not bookkeeping:

  • Later steps check against the brief. If the brief still says something the user has since overturned, the final check validates against a document everyone has stopped believing.
  • The reasoning evaporates otherwise. The chat is gone next week. The brief is what someone reads in six months to understand why the page looks like this.
  • An answer often changes more than the question it answered. In the capacity-cut run, "ignore AMs" did not just delete a constraint — it removed an entire approach, because that approach existed to be read by a captain and an AM together. Say that out loud. A silently shrinking option set looks like carelessness; an explained one is the method working.

Report it as a diff, not a redraft: what was open · what the answer is · what it changed.

IDs freeze once the brief is approved. Before approval, renumber freely. After it, a withdrawn constraint is marked withdrawn, never deleted, and new facts take new numbers. Renumbering after other documents reference C4 silently repoints every reference to something else — and nothing in the checks would catch it.

Section 2 — Shape

Starts only after the DRD is approved. This is where the real gate is.

Fetch once, here. One batched pass for everything the build needs — the screen, only the icons it references, the three fonts, kit.css, and any vendor script its markup links. Not before, not drip-fed.

All five approaches on one page, each a small schematic beside the captain's journey, what it does, what it costs, and what it rules out.

The rail is part of the schematic. It carries the real module names, and each card lights the module that approach actually lives in. Anonymous bars — or the same bar lit on every card — produce a placement comparison that says nothing about placement. See comparison-example.html.

Draw the situations, not only the happy path. Every situation the brief numbered gets a mark against each shape: lit where the shape has room to show it, faded where it does not. A shape that is blank in four situations out of seven is not a cheaper option, and the sheet is where that becomes visible rather than arguable.

Never skip this sheet because the DRD already drew a favourite. Liking an approach in the DRD is liking a paragraph. That is the thing this whole method exists to avoid — Section 1 says not to converge precisely because shapes argued as sentences lose to shapes seen side by side. A preference stated from prose is provisional until it has been looked at.

Build the sheet, show it, and let the preference survive or change. The user may still say "skip it, build A and B" — that is their call to make out loud. It is never yours to make on their behalf by reading ahead.

Build it with the wfc- classes in assets/wireframe.css; comparison-example.html is the standard.

  1. IA outline first — header, then sections in order, then what is in each. Check it against section-rules.md before drawing.
  2. Schematic, on purpose. The question here is which shape, not is this spacing right. Five full-size pages is too much scrolling to compare, and the rationale ends up nowhere near the shape it belongs to.
  3. Invent freely. The catalogue never limits what can be proposed. A method that only permits existing components cannot produce a new idea. Mark which blocks are new — those are the component backlog.
  1. The schematic is a contract with the design that follows. It must name the same blocks, in the same order, as the thing eventually built. If the design needs a block the schematic never showed, that is a change worth surfacing — not something to add quietly. A reviewer who picked a shape should recognise it when the design arrives; if they have to ask "which wireframe became this?", the schematic was decoration.
  1. Draw navigation as navigation. Tabs are a row with the active one underlined (wfc-tabs), never a row of equal bordered boxes — that reads as a button group and shows nothing about which view you are on. Use wfc-steps for a progression, never for navigation.

Gate — the reviewer picks one or two. Then build the real thing. There is no greyed full-size stage in between: once a shape is chosen, the next useful artefact is the design, and a full-size wireframe of a decided shape is a drawing of work already understood.

Losing approaches go to the Idea Wall (log-exploration): logged, never reviewed, allowed to go stale. Dead ends are the reason the wall exists.

Checking the design as UX, not only as UI

The layout checks are syntactic. They will all pass on a page nobody can use. Two things close that gap, and both have to be written, because a tick is one you award yourself.

Answer the eight principles in panel-principles.md, one short argument each, citing the design.

Then walk every situation. Not the happy path — every situation the edge sweep produced. The format is the same whatever the problem:

SITUATION   who they are, what state they are in, when they arrive
THEY WANT   the one thing, in their words
ARRIVING    what they already know, and what they just came from
  1 …       what they see · what they do · what they now believe
  2 …
OUTCOME     succeeds  ·  stalls at step N because ___

Four rules make it worth doing:

  1. One walkthrough per situation. If the sweep found six, write six. The happy path is the least informative of them.
  2. At least one where things have already gone wrong — the user is late, mistaken, or arriving mid-failure. Designs break there, not at step one.
  3. Phrase the question adversarially. "Is the hierarchy right?" grades itself; "what does this person get wrong?" has an answer.
  4. A stall is a finding, and it ships as one. Write it down even when you cannot fix it — an unfixed stall someone knows about beats a hidden one.

What this cannot do. A walkthrough written by whoever made the design is self-assessment. It catches the obvious stalls and misses the ones its author is blind to. It raises the floor; only a second reader raises the ceiling. Say so when handing the design over.

docs/page-framework.md — What a page is made of

The page framework

You asked whether a framework is needed. There already is one — it just was not written down. Every one of the 11 screens is the same skeleton with different contents, and there is exactly one documented exemption.

The skeleton

vp-shell                         the page. flex row, 100vh, never scrolls itself
├── vp-sidebar                   the rail. 250px, fixed, never scrolls
└── vp-content                   the work area. grey, scrolls, 16px side gutter
    ├── vp-pagebar               the header. sticky, min 52px, carries the h3
    ├── vp-tabs | vp-subtabbar    optional. sits flush under the header, 1px divider
    └── vp-panel                 the section. white, radius 8, 16px padding
        ├── section head (h4)    optional, may sit on a tinted strip
        └── content              cards, rows, tables, fields

vp-shell, vp-sidebar, vp-content, vp-pagebar and vp-panel each appear exactly 11 times across 11 screens — once per page, no exceptions. That is not a convention anyone has to remember; it is simply what a page is.

Heading levels carry meaning

Measured across every screen, with no counter-examples:

levelroleexample
h3page title, in the header bandPayments, Growth Dashboard, DC Capacity
h4section title, inside a panel or on its stripSet Your DC Capacity, Your Order Summary

There is no h1, h2, h5 or h6 anywhere in the panel. Do not introduce one — if content feels like it needs a third level, it is a card inside a section, not a heading.

Two screens title the page with the view rather than the module (Payment Details (17 Aug – 23 Aug)). That is correct when the page is a drill-down: the rail already says which module you are in.

How many sections a page has

Across all 11 screens: one or two. Never three.

That is a useful ceiling, not a hard law — but if a design wants four sections, that is strong evidence the page is really two pages, or that three of them are cards inside one section. Check section-rules.md before adding a third.

The one exemption

Service Area's three screens have no vp-panel at all. The map is the work area and runs edge to edge, with the 16px gutter deliberately bypassed (vp-content-flush). A map boxed inside a padded white card is a worse map.

This is the only exemption, it is deliberate, and it is documented so nobody "fixes" it. If a new page wants the same treatment, it needs the same justification: the content is the canvas.

Building order

Build in the order the skeleton nests. Starting from the content and working outwards is how pages end up with the gutter, the header height or the scroll container subtly wrong.

  1. shell + rail (copy from any existing screen — it is identical on all 11)
  2. header, with the h3
  3. tabs, if the module has them
  4. sections, top to bottom
  5. contents of each section

docs/layout-rules.md — Rail, header, work area

Universal layout rules

From the Guide for Slipstream frame in the Figma spacing file (node 28:13356). These hold for every module, not per screen. Until now they survived only as comments inside flow-ledger.html's stylesheet — which meant a new page could only be built correctly by someone who had read the CSS.

The structure these rules apply to is in page-framework.md.

Rail

  • 250px, fixed. Height follows the screen.
  • Never scrolls vertically. overflow: hidden, always.
  • 250 is our Crystal component's width, not the 224 the Figma guide draws. The guide is wrong here and the component is right — this was confirmed and is not up for re-deciding.

Header

  • Fixed to the top. Everything in the work area scrolls under it (position: sticky; top: 0).
  • 52px minimum, and it grows when its contents need more. The Figma guide draws 60 on a 1056 work area; 52 is the measured live value and wins.
  • Padding 12px 16px. It spans the full work area, cancelling the gutter with a negative inline margin, so the band runs edge to edge.

Work area

  • Always grey. Universally, every module.
  • 16px side gutter, 16px at the bottom, 0 at the top — the header provides the top edge.
  • The first section sits 16px below the header.
  • The work area is the scroll container, not the document.

Sections

  1. Each has a background colour. White unless something else is specified.
  2. 16px padding on all sides, for everything inside.
  3. 8px radius.
  4. 16px between stacked sections.
  5. A section may scroll independently of the page, vertically or horizontally.

Scrollbars

Whenever a section or the page can scroll, the scrollbar is visible by default. Nobody should have to guess that something scrolls. This is why the work area uses overflow-y: scroll rather than auto — a gutter that appears and disappears also shifts the layout by its own width.

The one place this does not apply is the map (below).

Tabs directly under the header

A tab strip immediately beneath the header does not take the 16px gap. The two bands meet on a 1px divider. Same for a sub-tab bar. Tabs always carry a white ground of their own rather than borrowing the work area's grey.

The map exemption

Service Area's three screens bypass the gutter entirely and run the map edge to edge, and the map's own scroll is panning, not scrolling — so the always-visible scrollbar rule does not apply to it either.

This is the only exemption. It is deliberate. Do not "fix" it.

Applying these to an existing screen

These rules were applied retroactively to every live-equivalent screen, with Service Area exempted. If you find a screen that disagrees with this file, the screen is wrong — but check deliberate differences first, because a few mismatches with live are intentional.

docs/section-rules.md — New section vs bake in

New section, or bake into an existing one?

The question that comes up on every new piece of work, and the one thing that was written down nowhere. The rule below is derived from the 11 screens already built, not invented — the worked examples are the evidence.

The test

Own heading + own data scope → its own section. An attribute of data already on the page → bake it in.

Both halves have to be true for a new section. A thing with a heading but no independent data is a card. A thing with its own data but no heading is a row.

Ask it in this order:

  1. Could this section be reloaded on its own? If its data comes from the same place as the section above it, it is not independent — bake it in.
  2. Does it need a title for the page to make sense? If you would not give it an h4, it is not a section.
  3. Is this the third section? No screen has three. See the ceiling below.

If you bake in, follow the hierarchy

Baked-in content must sit in the section's existing order, not get appended wherever it fits:

section head (h4) → context strip → the primary control or data → annotations

Dropping a new field below the save bar, or a new stat after the table, is the failure mode this rule exists to prevent. Match the hierarchy of the section you are joining — never something random.

The ceiling

One or two sections per page. Never three.

Across all 11 screens there is no counter-example. If a design calls for a third, one of these is true and worth deciding explicitly:

  • it is really two pages, and the rail should carry both; or
  • two of the three are cards inside one section; or
  • you have found the genuine first exception — in which case say so out loud and add it here, so the next person inherits the reasoning.

1. How it works

Two layers, the same shape as the colour foundation:

  • Scale — what a distance is. A 4px step ladder, plus negatives for deliberate pull-ins and overlaps: --vpSpace0 … --vpSpace40, --vpSpaceN4 … --vpSpaceN16.

2. The six roles

Straight from the Figma:

RoleMeans
marginscreen edge to content — the page's outer gutter
inlinebetween items sitting side by side, in a row
stackbetween items stacked one above the other
verPadinside a container, top and bottom
horPadinside a container, left and right
secGapbetween whole sections, where a divider usually sits

A shorthand is split by role: padding: A B is verPad A + horPad B; margin: A B is stack A + inline B. gap is stack in a column and inline in a row, read off the rule's own flex-direction.

What I got wrong, and the check that catches it

Every entry here is a real mistake made while building this catalog, and the specific check that would have caught it. Read this before starting a fidelity pass; run the checks in §7 before saying a page is done.

1. Measuring live

Never eyeball a comparison and call it superimposition. The divider under the sidebar's profile block went missing because "superimposition" meant a hand-picked list of elements plus a look at a screenshot. Replace it with an exhaustive inventory of both sides — walk every element, record geometry, type, colour, border, radius, padding, opacity — and diff the two lists programmatically.

Diff a component's inner elements, not just its bounding box. The Pilot Management banner's copy sat 20px left of live for a whole review cycle because the check listed the banner and its illustration but never the heading, body lines or button inside it. Everything on the list matched; the bug was in what was not listed.

Walk the full DOM depth. The segmented filter's outer stroke was missed because the check only walked three levels and live's container sits at the fourth.

Measure live before implementing, not after. The card radius was assumed 4px and is 8px. The footer blocks were assumed to reuse the 48px nav-row metrics and are 65 and 68. An inventory of the real values takes a minute and settles it.

Read computed styles before destroying the iframe. CSSStyleDeclaration is live; once the frame is removed every value reads back empty.

Do not trust getComputedStyle mid-transition. It reported the chevron's rotation as identity while the render was correct. For anything animated, check pixels.

Alpha colours read back lossy. A composited pixel at 20% opacity quantises: live's oklab(...) divider reads as #C3C8D2 off a canvas but converts to #C3C9D4 when the opaque oklab is converted. Convert the source value, don't sample the composite.

Wait for the map before capturing. Called out twice. Map screens need several seconds; confirm tiles have actually painted before screenshotting.

Don't guess a route. /captain/BEU/pilot-management works, /captain/BEU/service-area is a 403 while /captain/service-area/... is not. Navigate via the app's own nav.

Retry live's failures before concluding. The pilot table first rendered "having trouble showing this data" — one Retry loaded it.

2. When live disagrees with the design system

Live does not sit on the 4px grid, and its type and colour wander off Crystal. The standing decision is to extend the library, never snap the clone: 9 Valmo type styles on Crystal's 19, 5 Valmo colours, 15 Valmo spacing steps. Snapping would undo the pixel parity this catalog exists for. Label the additions so it stays obvious which values the design system sanctions and which live merely uses.

Exemptions already agreed, so they don't get re-litigated:

  • glyphs (▾ ‹ › + − ✓ ⓘ) sit outside the type library
  • artwork (map pins, shipment dots, legend keys) and alpha (shadows, scrims) sit outside the palette
  • the hero banner is exempt from the palette entirely — product may choose its own colours there, which is why its gradient is a literal

3. CSS traps in this codebase

A typo'd token silently no-ops. var(--vpSpacingInline20) did not exist, so the whole gap declaration was dropped and the value fell back to normal. Now that every distance routes through tokens this is the single most likely failure mode, and it fails invisibly. Run the undefined-var check after every CSS change.

An unterminated comment eats the rules after it. One missing */ in a generated token block swallowed the entire Valmo spacing scale; every padding collapsed to 0 and the page still looked plausible.

Inline styles in markup beat component CSS. A height:14px capped the footer logo; width/height: fit-content overrode the search field's metrics. When a component refuses to take a value, grep the markup for an inline override.

Check whether a class name is already taken. .vp-downloadbtn already belonged to the Service Area panel and was defined later in the sheet, so it quietly won.

Specificity beats intent. .vp-pageheader h3 (class+element) outranks a bare .vp-heading1, which is why the type utilities are scoped .vp-scope .vp-<name>.

Non-scaling strokes are already in screen pixels. Scaling stroke-width by 3.6 when porting SVG to Leaflet made every outline 4x too heavy.

Clipped geometry shows its seams once the view moves. Polygons clipped to a fixed viewport look fine on a static raster and become a hard rectangle on a pannable map.

4. Generated edits

Balance the counts after any scripted rewrite. A dropped </div> nested the whole Service Area panel inside a hidden layer and made it vanish; the naive check was fooled by a nested element's own closing tag. Count <div>/</div> and /*/*/ on the output, and assert.

Keep both copies of the seed in step. data-seed.json and the blob embedded in flow-ledger.html must be written together — updating one and then reading the other silently reverted three icons.

5. Refactor hygiene

Delete what a change orphans. .vp-pageheader and .vp-sa-title survived as dead code after the page band replaced them; page-header survived as a component no screen used, with the wrong title size, sitting next to its replacement.

Fix the metadata too. Screens kept naming a retired component in their components lists. That metadata is what the catalog and any agent reading manifest.json go by, so a stale name there is worse than a stale file.

The export only writes. It now prunes screens, components and icons the catalog no longer has, and fails if an <img> would ship without a src — added after a retired component's page lingered and after component markup referencing icons by key exported with no src at all.

6. Git and process

Squash-merging a stacked PR deletes its base branch and auto-closes the child, and a closed PR's base cannot be retargeted. Merge the base, rebase the child onto main, then merge — or use a merge commit.

Verify on the deployed build, not just locally. Serving locally proves the export; only the deployed URL proves what people will open.

Don't scan a JS application with an HTML-shaped regex. The ledger deliberately writes '<' + '/script>', sets src at runtime, and has /* inside strings — all of which trip naive structural checks. Verify functionally instead.

7. The checks to run before calling a page done

  1. Inventory live and ours exhaustively, including every component's inner elements, and diff programmatically.
  2. Superimpose live's real measured boxes over the rendered page.
  3. var() audit: every var(--vp…) referenced is defined. After the last CSS edit.
  4. Structure: <div>/</div> and /*/*/ balance on every exported page.
  5. No raw px in any padding/margin/gap; no colour outside the palette; no type outside the library — except the documented exemptions.
  6. Every image loads; no <img> without a src.
  7. All 34 pages render with no console errors.
  8. Re-verify on the deployed URL after merging.

8. One stylesheet, not thirty-four copies

Screens and components link assets/kit.css; they do not carry their own copy of the kit. Until 2026-09-15 each of the 34 pages inlined the whole 62KB kit, which meant:

  • a one-line change to a shared token rewrote all 34 pages (~2MB of duplicated CSS),
  • PR diffs were 36 files wide for a single rule, and

Capacity Cut for Latent

The KRD this demo run was built from, as received. Converted from Demo 1 Capacity cut for latent.docx with nothing added or removed.

The capacity cut framework penalises any Latent DC that fails to hold performance or leans on high-productivity pilots. It runs on the same process as Valmo and is controlled entirely by the central Control Tower

Benchmark

Each Latent DC is benchmarked against Valmo at hex level, on the hexes the Latent DC serves. Comparing the same hexes removes the area mix effect that a pin-level comparison carries. Each DC is allowed a delta above the benchmark. The delta is relative, not absolute, so it adjusts for seasonality and does not penalise a DC for a network-wide movement it does not control.The delta tightens as a DC matures, because Latent pilots deliver for the first time and are expected to close the gap on the learning curve.

  • Mature DCs (8+ weeks) hold a 2pp delta.
  • Stable DCs (5–8 weeks) hold a 5pp delta.
  • New DCs (under 5 weeks) run on a soft target and are outside the cut.

If cuts do not prove effective and the gap still carries a high impact on RTO, we will tighten the thresholds further.

Metrics and thresholds

COD RTO is lagging, so the weekly check runs on leading indicators that flag a breach before RTO reflects it, in line with how Valmo runs. Misroute is now a separate metric, and productivity is added to protect the Latent persona.

MetricMeasured asStableMature
D0 attempt%Delta with Valmo D0 attempt on the same hexes the Latent DC serves5pp2pp
COD FAD%Delta with Valmo COD FAD on the same hexes the Latent DC serves5pp2pp
Misroute within polygon%Misroute marked inside the polygon, vs Valmo misroute on the same hexes the Latent DC serves15% above Valmo15% above Valmo
Pilot Load%Active FEs crossing 280 weekly OFD20% of active FEs20% of active FEs
  • Misroute carries a 15% allowance because a Latent polygon is small, so the chance of a wrong area prediction is higher than on Valmo.
  • Productivity protects the persona. Latent pilots are part-time, earning ₹8,000–11,000 a month, and 280 weekly OFD is derived backwards from that band. A DC leaning on a few high-volume pilots carries churn risk, because competition can outbid us on earnings and the remaining pilots cannot clear the load. No pilot is blocked, since pilots do not control their assignment. The cut acts at DC level, where the staffing decision sits, and DC captains are excluded from the count. Both 280 and 20% are configurable per DC.

How the cut works

  • A breach on any metric triggers a 20% capacity cut. The cut is 20% irrespective of which metric breached, and cuts never stack within a week, because every breach points to the same manpower gap.
  • Cuts continue weekly to a floor of 100 orders (MVV).

Reinstatement

  • Capacity is reinstated by 25% only when every metric is green. Reinstatement restores the DC to its pre-cut capacity. A 20% cut takes capacity from x to 0.8x; restoring 25% of 0.8x returns it to x.
  • For performance metrics, green means the metric is back within threshold.
  • For pilot load%, green means the DC has onboarded a new FE (100+ orders and 4+ active days in the week). Productivity metric also falls if the DC's volume falls, since fewer orders means fewer FEs cross 280 OFD. That is not recovery. Green therefore requires a newly onboarded FE, not a lower reading.
  • A single red metric blocks reinstatement.
  • If any metric is still red when the cooldown ends, the DC takes another 20% cut.

Ownership with Control Tower

  • Parallel engine on the Valmo sheet. We have built a dedicated Latent model that computes cuts for Latent DCs and appends them to the same capacity-cut sheet as Valmo. Actioning follows the Valmo process end to end.
  • Exceptions only through CT. There is no kill switch with the Latent ops team. Any foreseeable exception, such as sale and festive days, and any unforeseen event or late actioning, such as rain, is raised by the Latent ops team to CT. CT holds final authority to implement it.
  • Alignment. The model has been merged with the Valmo capacity-cut flow and aligned with CT. We will reconvene with CT on the stricter thresholds and the productivity metric, after which the full framework runs with the central team.

Visibility

A tab on the captain panel will go live in October, before enforcement. It shows FE-level and DC-level metrics, the threshold for each, any warning, and the reason for any cut, so captains and AMs know the policy before it acts on them. Pilots receive no communication, since they cannot act on it.

Brief — Capacity Cut for Latent

Step 1 of 4. Nothing is designed yet and nothing has been fetched. This exists so every later step can be checked against it by name.

The problem, in one sentence

A Latent DC loses a fifth of its capacity for breaching any one of four weekly metrics, and today finds out only after it has happened.

The single decision the page must support: what do I do this week so I am not cut — or so I get my capacity back?

What it touches

dc-capacity owns the number the cut acts on · growth-dashboard already explains why orders moved and runs weekly, like the cut · pilot-management owns the roster, and staffing is the only lever in the captain's own hands.

Constraints

These come from the KRD. Every later step has to account for them by name.

constraint
C1A breach of any one metric triggers a flat 20% cut. Cuts never stack within a week.
C2Cuts continue weekly down to a floor of 100 orders.
C3Reinstatement is +25% of the cut capacity — which restores it in full — and only when every metric is green.
C4For Pilot Load, green means a newly onboarded FE, not a lower reading. A falling order count lowers the reading without anything improving.
C5The captain cannot turn this off. Exceptions go through Control Tower only.
C6The allowance tightens with maturity: new DCs under 5 weeks are exempt, stable 5pp, mature 2pp.
C7Pilots receive no communication about this at all.
C8AMs are out of scope. The KRD names them, but nothing is built for them here. Confirmed, not assumed.

Success test

The page is right if these are true of a real person using it. They are checked again, line by line, against the built design in step 4.

the page is right if…
T1a captain under a cut can say which metric caused it, without asking anyone
T2a captain can say exactly what has to happen for capacity to come back — including that a lower Pilot Load reading is not enough (C4)
T3a captain not yet cut can tell whether they are close to one
T4a captain cannot mistake this for a place to dispute the cut (C5)

What the panel already tells us

From panel-principles.md, and each one changes something here:

  • The panels are merging — design for a captain working here all day under operational pressure, not an end-of-day reporter.
  • – in an empty cell reads as a loading failure. Captains see a bare dash and conclude the page is broken, not that there is genuinely nothing there. Our own dc-capacity does this today — the action bar shows - until a number is typed. Wherever a value is legitimately absent, say so in words.
  • Selected and unselected states are already reported as indistinguishable. Anything tabbed has to do more than change font weight.
  • Recall is not available. S1 has never seen this and will not have been trained. The page explains itself or it fails.
  • WhatsApp is the competitor. If an AM can explain this faster in a message, the page has not earned its place.

Approaches — Capacity Cut for Latent

Step 2 of 4. Five approaches. Two changes since the last draft, both worth stating rather than quietly applying:

  • A scorecard for a captain and an AM to read together was removed. C8 settled that nothing is built for AMs, so it had no second reader and no reason to exist.
  • Growth Dashboard was missing and is back as E. It was an approach in the first draft; when this set was rebuilt around the axes it disappeared, and nothing said so. That is a silent shrink, which is precisely the failure the method warns about. Caught on review, not by me. Each is placed on the design axes, says which

success tests it passes, and names the constraints it leans on.

The axes

Placement is how you get into a page; these are the choices inside it. A set clustered on one point is thin, and the table is what shows that.

They are genuinely different by the discriminator — switching between any two changes what the captain actually does: review and plan (A), react while committing a number (B), onboard an FE (C), wait to be told (D), and understand why last week went the way it did (E).

Placement is an axis too — it is just not the only one. The previous draft over-corrected: having said placement is not the design axis, it dropped placement variation altogether, and a whole module went with it. A and B sit in DC Capacity, C in Pilot Management, D in the notification layer, E in Growth Dashboard. Vary placement and the rest.

The framing span was also short. It read status · warning · instruction · score. E is none of those — it is an explanation, which is a fifth framing and the one Growth Dashboard exists to provide.

The approaches

A · A "Capacity Cut" tab in DC Capacity

What the captain does

  1. A weekly notice arrives, or they open DC Capacity themselves.
  2. They tap the Capacity Cut tab.
  3. They read the cut — 20% — and which metric caused it.
  4. They scroll to the list of field executives.
  5. They see a new FE has to be hired. A lower number will not do.

Two sections: this week's four metrics against their thresholds, and the field executives over 280 weekly OFD.

Wins when the cut is understood as a capacity event, and the captain is the one who acts. Costs a second screen. Rules out nothing. Leans on C1, C3, C4, C6.

Why it can work. The cut is a capacity event, and this puts it where capacity already lives — the captain does not have to learn a new place. A tab costs nothing structurally: two modules already use them, and the module's own page stays exactly as it is. Most of all it is the only shape with room for a second section, which is what the hiring rule needs: a number cannot carry it, a list of people can.

What would make it fail. DC Capacity is not a page captains open daily. On its own it waits to be found — which is why it needs a door, not why it is the wrong home.

B · An alert where the capacity number is committed

What the captain does

  1. They open DC Capacity to set next week's number.
  2. They type a capacity figure.
  3. An alert tells them a cut is already active.
  4. They stop there. Nothing on the page says how to undo it.

No new screen. An inline alert on Set Your DC Capacity, at the moment the captain types a number.

Wins when the goal is to stop a bad commitment rather than to explain a policy. Costs everything that does not fit in an alert — the FE table cannot go here, so T2 is only half answered. Leans on C1, C5.

Why it can work. It is the only shape that reaches the captain at the moment of the decision, rather than after it. Nothing is navigated to and nothing is learned: the alert finds them while they are already typing a number, which is the one moment the cut has direct consequences.

What would make it fail. An alert is a sentence. The hiring rule needs a list, and there is nowhere under a form field to put one. It also has nothing to say to a captain who has not been cut, which is most captains most weeks.

C · Pilot-first, on the roster

What the captain does

  1. They open Pilot Management.
  2. A banner says Pilot Load has breached.
  3. They read the two new columns, one field executive at a time.
  4. They see exactly who is over 280.

Two columns on Pilot Management — weekly OFD and over/under 280 — and a banner when Pilot Load is breached.

Wins when the belief is that staffing is the only real lever, so put the metric next to it. Costs the other three metrics, which have nowhere to go. Rules out T1 and T3. Leans on C4 — and it is the only approach that makes the hiring rule concrete, because the roster is where a new FE actually appears.

Why it can work. Pilot Load is a problem about people, and this is the only shape that shows people. A captain reading 9 of 34 over 280 knows something a percentage cannot tell them — and the roster is the one screen where hiring a new FE visibly changes the picture, because that is where a new FE appears.

What would make it fail. It explains one metric out of four. A captain cut for D0 attempt would find nothing here, and would have no reason to look.

D · A pushed weekly notice

What the captain does

  1. The notice arrives at the start of the week.
  2. They read which metric broke.
  3. They tap through to the Capacity Cut tab.

The panel's notification module carries the breach; the detail lives wherever it already lives.

Wins when the problem is that nobody looks in time. It is the only approach that does not require the captain to come. Costs everything before the breach — it cannot answer T3, because it only exists once the cut has happened. Leans on C1, C5.

Why it can work. It is the only shape that does not wait to be found. Captains live in WhatsApp and check the panel when something sends them there, so a weekly notice matches how the news actually travels today — and the panel is where it should land.

What would make it fail. Every module already nudges, and nothing says which matters. Without a reason to outrank the others and a date to stop, this becomes one more thing to dismiss.

E · A reason on Growth Dashboard

What the captain does

  1. They open Growth Dashboard, which they do anyway.
  2. They see orders are down.
  3. A card says a capacity cut is the reason.
  4. They tap through to the Capacity Cut tab.

The cut appears as one more entry in Your Order Summary's reasons, beside the capacity card already there.

Wins when the captain's real question is why did last week go the way it did — and that is the question they already arrive at Growth Dashboard holding. It is the module most likely to be open, it is weekly like the cut, and it already names capacity as a reason orders moved.

Costs everything after the explanation. Growth Dashboard diagnoses and points elsewhere, so it can say a cut happened but never what to do about it — and it cannot say that a lower reading is not enough, which is the subtlest thing in the KRD (C4).

Rules out T2. Would be a third section on a module that already has two, which section-rules.md says no screen does. Leans on C1, C6.

Why it can work. Growth Dashboard is the page captains already open, and the question it answers — why did orders drop? — is the exact question a cut creates. The cut is one reason among the reasons already shown there, so it needs no new habit and no new place.

What would make it fail. It can name the cause and nothing else. If the link does not land the captain inside the detail, it tells them they have a problem and leaves them there.

Open question — where should this live?

Recommended: A, with two doors. The deciding reason is unchanged: the cut acts on the capacity number, so it belongs where the capacity number lives. Growth Dashboard gets a door, and dc-capacity carries a notice when a cut is live.

A is still the only approach that passes all four tests, and the deciding reason is unchanged: the cut acts on the capacity number, so it belongs where that number lives.

But E changes the shape of the recommendation rather than the answer. E is the best discovery surface we have and the worst home — most likely to be open, weekly like the cut, already answering the adjacent question, and structurally unable to say what to do about any of it. That is the definition of a door rather than a home, and now it is written down as a rejected approach with its reason, instead of being a decision I made quietly while redrawing a table.

So: A, with two doors — the capacity card on Growth Dashboard links to the tab (E as a door), and dc-capacity carries a notice when a cut is live. D also becomes a door rather than an approach.

Three things the drawings showed that the writing did not:

Only A has something to show when nothing is wrong. B, C and D only exist once a cut has happened. So they are blank for a captain who has never been cut (S1), one who is close to the line (S2), and a new DC that is exempt (S6). B cannot answer T3 for the same reason — a captain cannot ask "am I close?" on a screen that appears only after the answer is yes. This is what makes A the home. Not the score.

B beat C. In writing, C sounded like the better idea. Drawn, B answers two tests and C answers one. Both are still out. But they were ranked the wrong way round, and C was the one carried forward.

E is thin, and that is the risk. One test out of four. E can say a cut caused the drop. It cannot say what to do next. Everything depends on its link opening the Capacity Cut tab. If that link is weak, E is decoration.

Still carried, still unresolved

S5 — every metric green, but no new FE. Only C made this impossible to miss, because it lists people instead of showing a percentage. C is out. A picks this up through its field-executive list. That list is now doing real work: it is the only place the hiring rule becomes visible — capacity returns only when a new FE is hired (C4). If the design weakens the list, S5 goes with it.
